## Recording Text

Hi everyone,

today I want to talk about the TikTok algorithm. More exactly, I want to explain how the For You page works, why it can feel addictive, and why this topic is important for teenagers.

TikTok is not just an app where we watch random short videos. It is also a prediction system. While we are using it, the app learns from our behaviour. Every swipe, pause, like, search, follow, comment, and skip can become a signal for the algorithm.

The most important part of TikTok is the For You page. This is the feed where one video starts after another. Two people can open TikTok at the same time and see completely different videos. That happens because the For You page is personalised for each user.

First, I want to explain what an algorithm actually is. An algorithm is basically a set of rules or calculations that a computer uses to make decisions. In the case of TikTok, the decision is simple but powerful. Which video should be shown next?

TikTok says that the For You page uses different kinds of signals. On my first graph, I show three main groups of signals. The strongest group is user interactions. This includes likes, comments, shares, follows, searches, watch time, completion, rewatching, and skipping. The second group is video information, for example captions, hashtags, sounds, topics, and the length of the video. The third group is device and account settings, like language, country, age, and device type.

The graph is not an exact formula, because TikTok does not publish the exact weights of its algorithm. But TikTok does say that active interest signals are more important than basic settings. For example, watching a longer video until the end is a stronger signal than just having the same country setting as the creator.

This is important because attention itself becomes data. Sometimes we watch a video not because we really like it, but because it is shocking, funny, dramatic, or weird. The algorithm may still understand that the video kept our attention. So the feed is not only based on what we consciously choose. It is also based on what we spend time on.

A simple way to understand the algorithm is like a loop. On my feedback-loop graph, the process has five steps. First, the user watches or skips a video. Second, TikTok measures signals from that behaviour. Third, the algorithm predicts what could be interesting next. Fourth, the feed changes and shows a new video. Fifth, the user watches again, and the loop starts again.

This is why TikTok can learn very quickly. The videos are short, so a user can watch many videos in only a few minutes. Every video gives the system new information. If someone watches football videos to the end, skips cooking videos, and searches for gym routines, the algorithm can learn this pattern very fast.

My second graph shows daily platform use among teenagers in the United States. According to Pew Research Center data from 2025, 61 percent of United States teens said they use TikTok daily. YouTube was even higher, but TikTok was still one of the most visited platforms. This data is not from Germany, so we should be careful with it. But it shows that recommendation systems are a normal part of many teenagers' everyday life.

Now I want to explain why TikTok can feel addictive.

The first reason is infinite scroll. There is no real ending. When you finish a film, the film is over. When you read a chapter, the chapter ends. But on TikTok, the feed just continues. There is always one more video. This makes stopping harder, because the app never says that you are finished.

The second reason is autoplay. The next video starts automatically. You do not have to make a decision every time. This sounds like a small detail, but it is powerful. If I had to click play for every video, I would think more about whether I really want to continue. But if the next video starts by itself, I can keep watching without thinking much.

The third reason is the reward system. The next video might be boring, but it might also be exactly what I want to see. It might be funny, interesting, emotional, surprising, or useful. This is called a variable reward. The reward is not guaranteed, but it can happen at any moment. That uncertainty keeps people curious and makes them swipe again.

The fourth reason is personalisation. TikTok does not only show random videos. It learns what catches my attention. After some time, the feed can feel like it knows me. It can show my hobbies, my humour, my favourite music, my opinions, or even my insecurities. This makes the reward stronger, because the content feels personal.

Another point is social feedback. TikTok is full of likes, comments, trends, challenges, duets, and sounds. People do not only watch videos. They also see what other people are reacting to. This can create a feeling that something is happening right now and that you do not want to miss it.

Researchers often talk about a state called flow. Flow means that someone is very focused and forgets the time. This can happen in sports, games, music, or creative work. But it can also happen while scrolling. Because TikTok is fast, easy to use, and very personalised, people can lose track of time. Maybe they only wanted to watch for five minutes, but suddenly half an hour is gone.

On my risk map, I show four features that are often connected with addictive design concerns. These are infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications, and highly personalised recommendations. The European Commission also mentioned these features in February 2026 in a preliminary finding under the Digital Services Act. Preliminary means that it was not the final legal result. But it shows that governments are taking the topic seriously, especially when young users are involved.

However, I think it is important to stay fair. It would be too simple to say that TikTok is evil and everyone is addicted. That is not true. Many people use TikTok for fun, creativity, learning, comedy, news, sports, music, and community. Small creators can reach a big audience, even if they do not have many followers.

The problem is not that TikTok is entertaining. The problem is that the app is designed to keep attention for as long as possible. This can become a conflict. The app wants me to stay, but maybe I actually wanted to do homework, sleep, meet friends, or take a break from my phone.

There are also risks. One risk is that users spend more time on the app than planned. Another risk is sleep. If someone scrolls late at night, it can affect rest and concentration the next day. For students, this can become a real problem, because school needs attention and time.

Another risk is the filter bubble. If the algorithm learns that I like one type of content, it may show me more and more of the same thing. That can be harmless with sports or comedy. But it can be problematic if the content is about extreme opinions, unrealistic beauty standards, dieting, stress, or negative emotions. Then the feed can make a topic feel bigger and more normal than it really is.

TikTok also has tools that users can use. For example, users can press Not interested, manage topics, filter keywords, refresh the feed, use screen-time settings, or turn off notifications. These tools can help, but they only work if people actually use them.

In my opinion, the most important thing is that users understand how the feed works. If I watch a topic for a long time, TikTok may show me more of it. If I do not want that, I should not only think that I did not like the video. I should also remember that watching it is already a signal.

At the end of my website, I listed the sources for my graphs and information. I used TikTok's own explanation of the For You page, TikTok's Safety Center, Pew Research Center data about teenagers, a European Commission statement about addictive design concerns, and academic research about short-video addiction.

To conclude, TikTok's algorithm is not magic. It is a feedback loop between human attention and machine prediction. The app shows videos, we react, the algorithm learns, and the next videos become more personal. This can be useful and entertaining, but it can also make the app hard to stop using.

So my final answer is this. TikTok is not automatically bad, but it is also not neutral. It is designed to keep us engaged. That is why we should not only ask, do I like this video? We should also ask, did I choose to watch this, or did the algorithm choose it for me?
